Structure factors: S(q), X-ray and neutron

At low q the exact sum over every reciprocal-lattice vector the cell allows; above the switch-over, the Fourier transform of the partial pair distributions with a Lorch window against truncation ripples. X-ray and neutron patterns weight the partials by form factors or scattering lengths (Faber–Ziman).

$$\begin{gathered}S(q) = 1 + \frac{\tfrac{1}{N}\left|\sum_j w_j e^{i q\cdot r_j}\right|^2 - \langle w^2\rangle}{\langle w\rangle^2} \\ S_{ab}(q) - 1 = 4\pi\rho\int r^2\left(g_{ab}(r) - 1\right)\frac{\sin qr}{qr}\,L(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\end{gathered}$$

Faber & Ziman 1965; Lorch 1969

Free volume and pore sizes

Probe insertion on a grid: a point is free when a probe of the given radius centred there overlaps no van der Waals sphere. Bondi's fractional free volume uses the occupied van der Waals volume; each free point's pore size is the largest atom-free sphere containing it.

$$\mathrm{FFV} = 1 - \frac{1.3\,V_w}{V}, \qquad \text{PSD:}\ \ d(x) = \max\left\{2R : x \in \text{sphere}(c, R) \text{ free of atoms}\right\}$$

Bondi 1964; Gelb & Gubbins 1999

Mean-square displacement and diffusion

Displacements of atoms and of molecule centres over every time origin, with the drift of the whole system removed. The diffusion coefficient comes from the linear part of the molecule-centre MSD. Given the shear viscosity and the temperature, the Yeh–Hummer finite-size correction is added.

$$\begin{gathered}D_\mathrm{PBC} = \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\langle|r(t) - r(0)|^2\right\rangle}{6t}, \qquad \text{fitted over 20–50\% of the run} \\ D_\infty = D_\mathrm{PBC} + \frac{k_\mathrm{B}T\,\xi}{6\pi\eta L}, \qquad \xi = 2.837297\end{gathered}$$

Einstein 1905; Yeh & Hummer 2004

Adsorption locator

Low-energy places of adsorbate molecules on a fixed substrate (a crystal surface, a filler, a polymer surface or cell) by Monte Carlo simulated annealing. The adsorbates, built from SMILES and added after the substrate, move as rigid bodies: translations, rotations about their centre and jumps to a random place and orientation, accepted by the Metropolis rule at a temperature falling geometrically from the start to the end value within each cycle; jumps fade out as it cools, and each cycle ends with a downhill polish of its lowest configuration with shrinking steps; later cycles restart from the best so far. The energy is the assigned force field's non-bonded interaction between molecules.

$$\begin{gathered}E_\mathrm{ads} = \sum_\text{adsorbates} E(\text{adsorbate, substrate}) + \sum_\text{pairs} E(\text{adsorbate, adsorbate}) \\ \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}N_i} = \left\langle E(\text{one molecule of } i, \text{ everything else})\right\rangle, \qquad P_\mathrm{acc} = \min\left(1,\, e^{-\Delta E/kT}\right)\end{gathered}$$

Kirkpatrick, Gelatt & Vecchi 1983; Metropolis et al. 1953

Sorption: Widom insertion and grand-canonical Monte Carlo

How much of a gas or small molecule a fixed host takes up — a polymer or rubber cell, a filler, a porous crystal. The sorbate is a molecule typed with the host's force field, rigid or bending at its rotatable bonds. Widom test-particle insertion averages the Boltzmann factor of one molecule placed at random places and orientations: the excess chemical potential, the Henry constant and the solubility coefficient follow. Grand-canonical Monte Carlo at each pressure (insertions, deletions, translations and rotations; an ideal-gas reservoir, fugacity = pressure) gives the loading, its error from ten blocks, and the isosteric heat from the fluctuations of energy and number. A gas mixture is several rigid sorbates at once, each at its share of the pressure (f_i = y_i p): insertions and deletions pick a gas at random, and each gas gets its loading, its Widom terms alone, its isosteric heat from the multicomponent fluctuations, and the adsorption selectivity over the first gas. A rigid model (TraPPE's CO₂, N₂, O₂: bond and angle constants 0) takes its force field's own bond lengths and angles before the run (TraPPE CO₂: C=O 1.16 Å, linear); with a united-atom host the sorbate keeps its types' own charges (TraPPE CO₂ +0.70/−0.35 e). A sorbate gets its force field's own preparation (united-atom sites; a model's massless charge sites such as TraPPE N₂'s centre, added as virtual sites). A bending sorbate is cut at its rotatable bonds (single, acyclic, not amide or ester) into rigid pieces and grown into the host piece by piece by configurational bias: the first piece at k random places and orientations, each later piece at k torsion angles uniform on the circle, each trial weighted by the Boltzmann factor of its energy with the host, the other sorbates and the atoms already placed (1-4 pairs scaled) plus the torsion terms about that bond; one trial is drawn by its weight and the product of the mean weights is the molecule's Rosenbluth weight W. The same growth with nothing around it gives the isolated molecule's ⟨W_IG⟩, the reference of every Widom average and GCMC acceptance, so μ_ex, the Henry constant and the isotherm stay exact for any number of trials. Deletions retrace the molecule with its own conformation as one of the trials; partial regrowth beyond a random rotatable bond and twists of one bond change its conformation in place. A held loading runs canonical Monte Carlo at a set number of molecules (grown in by configurational bias, then moved, turned, regrown and twisted): the loaded cell's energy and its parts, the adsorption energy per molecule (the molecules' own torsional energy in the gas subtracted), the distribution of single molecules' binding energies, the lowest-energy arrangement met and a density map; the loaded cell can be kept as the structure.

$$\begin{gathered}\mu_\mathrm{ex} = -kT\ln\langle e^{-\beta\Delta U}\rangle, \qquad K_H = \frac{\langle W\rangle\,V}{RT\,m_\mathrm{host}}, \qquad S = \frac{\langle W\rangle\,T_0}{p_0\,T} \\ P_\mathrm{ins} = \min\left(1,\, \frac{\beta f V}{N + 1}\,e^{-\beta\Delta U}\right), \qquad Q_\mathrm{st} = kT - \frac{\langle UN\rangle - \langle U\rangle\langle N\rangle}{\langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2} \\ f_i = y_i\,p, \qquad S_{i/1} = \frac{x_i/x_1}{y_i/y_1}, \qquad q_i = kT - \sum_j \operatorname{cov}(U, N_j)\,\left[\operatorname{cov}(N, N)^{-1}\right]_{ji} \\ W = \prod_l \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} e^{-\beta u_{lj}}, \qquad \mu_\mathrm{ex} = -kT\ln\frac{\langle W\rangle}{\langle W_\mathrm{IG}\rangle}, \qquad P_\mathrm{ins} = \min\left(1,\, \frac{\beta f V}{N+1}\,\frac{W}{\langle W_\mathrm{IG}\rangle}\right), \qquad P_\mathrm{del} = \min\left(1,\, \frac{N}{\beta f V}\,\frac{\langle W_\mathrm{IG}\rangle}{W}\right) \\ P_\mathrm{regrow} = \min\left(1,\, \frac{W_\mathrm{new}}{W_\mathrm{old}}\right), \qquad Q_\mathrm{st} = kT + \langle u_\mathrm{intra}\rangle_\mathrm{IG} - \frac{\operatorname{cov}(U, N)}{\operatorname{var}(N)}, \qquad E_\mathrm{ads} = \frac{\langle U\rangle - \sum_i N_i\,\langle u_{\mathrm{intra},i}\rangle_\mathrm{IG}}{N}\end{gathered}$$

Widom 1963; Frenkel & Smit 2002; Siepmann & Frenkel 1992

Shear viscosity by Green–Kubo

The shear viscosity is the time integral of the pressure tensor's autocorrelation in equilibrium. CAPS runs NVT dynamics of a copy of the frame (Nosé–Hoover, weakly coupled so the dynamics are nearly Newtonian), samples the full pressure tensor (kinetic and virial) every few femtoseconds, and averages the autocorrelation of all five independent components of its traceless symmetric part. The running integral should level off; its mean over the last third of the window is the value, and blocks of the run give the error. The result feeds the Yeh–Hummer finite-size correction of diffusion coefficients.

$$\eta = \frac{V}{10\,k_\mathrm{B}T}\int_0^\infty \sum_{\alpha\beta}\left\langle P^\circ_{\alpha\beta}(0)\,P^\circ_{\alpha\beta}(t)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t, \qquad P^\circ_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{P_{\alpha\beta} + P_{\beta\alpha}}{2} - \delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\frac{\operatorname{tr}P}{3}$$

Daivis & Evans 1994

Elastic constants by static strain

The minimised cell is strained by ±ε along each of the six components, re-minimised with the pair list frozen, and the stress read from the virial; C_ij are the central differences. Isotropic moduli are Hill averages of the Voigt and Reuss bounds.

$$C_{ij} = -\frac{\sigma_i(+\epsilon_j) - \sigma_i(-\epsilon_j)}{2\epsilon}, \qquad K_H,\ G_H = \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{Voigt} + \text{Reuss})$$

Elastic constants from stress fluctuations

At finite temperature the elastic constants are the Born term (second strain derivatives of the energy) minus the stress fluctuations, plus the kinetic term, averaged over an NVT run.

$$C_{ijkl} = \langle C^\mathrm{B}_{ijkl}\rangle - \frac{V}{k_\mathrm{B}T}\left(\langle\sigma_{ij}\sigma_{kl}\rangle - \langle\sigma_{ij}\rangle\langle\sigma_{kl}\rangle\right) + \frac{N k_\mathrm{B}T}{V}\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right)$$

Lutsko 1989; Clavier et al. 2017. The kinetic term has this form because σ is the virial stress alone; a stress with its kinetic part would change it

χ from pair contacts

The Flory–Huggins χ(T) of two components from the energies of their molecules in contact. Each component is one rigid molecule (a solvent, or a repeat unit with its ends capped by hydrogen). Random relative orientations and directions are drawn; the second molecule slides in until the van der Waals surfaces touch, and the pair energy is recorded. The energies are Boltzmann-averaged at each temperature, the coordination numbers come from packing as many molecules as fit around one, and χ(T) is fitted to A + B/T.

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta E_\mathrm{mix}(T) = \frac{1}{2}\left[Z_{AB}\langle E_{AB}\rangle_T + Z_{BA}\langle E_{BA}\rangle_T - Z_{AA}\langle E_{AA}\rangle_T - Z_{BB}\langle E_{BB}\rangle_T\right] \\ \langle E\rangle_T = \frac{\sum E\,e^{-E/RT}}{\sum e^{-E/RT}}, \qquad \chi(T) = \frac{\Delta E_\mathrm{mix}}{RT}\end{gathered}$$

Fan, Olafson, Blanco & Hsu 1992

Polyhedral template matching

Each particle's nearest neighbours, with the particle itself, are compared with ideal FCC, HCP, BCC, icosahedral and simple-cubic neighbourhoods. Both point sets are centred and scaled to unit mean distance; neighbours are matched to template points from anchor pairs whose angles agree, and the optimal rotation (Horn's quaternion) gives the RMSD. The template with the least RMSD below the cutoff is the particle's structure; its rotation is the lattice orientation, and the deformation left after it gives a shear strain.

$$\begin{gathered}\mathrm{RMSD} = \min_R\sqrt{\frac{\sum_k\left|R\hat{v}_k - \hat{t}_{\pi(k)}\right|^2}{N + 1}}, \qquad F = \left(\sum x\,t^\mathrm{T}\right)\left(\sum t\,t^\mathrm{T}\right)^{-1} \\ E = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(F^\mathrm{T}F - I\right), \qquad \epsilon_\mathrm{shear} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\sum\operatorname{dev}(E)^2}\end{gathered}$$

Larsen, Schmidt & Schiøtz 2016

Infrared spectrum from the dipole

Pore network

The geometry a spherical probe sees in a periodic cell. On a grid, CAPS computes the distance from every point to the nearest van der Waals surface. The points farther than the probe radius are the space the probe's centre can reach. They are joined into clusters through the grid's faces and across the periodic boundaries. A cluster that reaches its own image percolates along those axes and is a channel; one that does not is a pocket, closed to anything coming from outside. LCD is the largest included sphere. The pore-limiting diameter PLD along each axis is twice the largest radius r for which the points farther than r from every surface still percolate along that axis, found by bisection. Dif is the largest included sphere along that free path. The surface is sampled with an even Fibonacci lattice of points on every atom's sphere of radius R + probe. Powder pattern: reflections and I(2θ)

The diffraction pattern a powder of the cell gives on a laboratory diffractometer or with neutrons, from the cell's reciprocal lattice. Every vector G = h b₁ + k b₂ + l b₃ up to 4π sin θ_max/λ gets its structure factor from the atoms' Cromer–Mann form factors (or coherent neutron lengths) with a temperature factor e^(−Bs²); |F|² is averaged over the frames, so an MD run brings its own thermal damping and diffuse scattering. Vectors of equal |G| make one reflection, their number its multiplicity; absences follow from F. Each line of the source (Kα1, and Kα2 at half its intensity) places the reflection by Bragg's law; its integrated intensity is m|F|² times the Lorentz–polarisation factor (with a monochromator's angle) and, for an oriented sample, the March–Dollase factor of each vector. Peaks are pseudo-Voigt functions of area one: the Gaussian width from the instrument (Caglioti U, V, W) and microstrain 4ε tan θ, the Lorentzian width from Kλ/(L cos θ) (Scherrer; one crystallite size or one per cell axis) and X tan θ + Y/cos θ, combined by Thompson, Cox and Hastings; a Gaussian, a Lorentzian or a fixed-η pseudo-Voigt can be chosen instead. Works on a crystal's unit cell and on any periodic MD cell; a measured pattern can be drawn over it.

$$\begin{gathered}F(\mathbf{G}) = \sum_j f_j(s)\,e^{-Bs^2}\,e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}_j}, \qquad \lambda|\mathbf{G}| = 4\pi\sin\theta, \qquad I = m\,\overline{|F|^2}\;LP\;P_\mathrm{MD} \\ LP = \frac{1 + \cos^2 2\theta_m \cos^2 2\theta}{(1 + \cos^2 2\theta_m)\,\sin^2\theta\cos\theta}, \qquad P_\mathrm{MD} = \left(r^2\cos^2\alpha + \frac{\sin^2\alpha}{r}\right)^{-3/2} \\ H_G^2 = U\tan^2\theta + V\tan\theta + W + (4\varepsilon\tan\theta)^2, \qquad H_L = X\tan\theta + \frac{Y}{\cos\theta} + \frac{K\lambda}{L\cos\theta}\end{gathered}$$

Fitting a measured powder pattern (Rietveld, Pawley)

A measured powder pattern (2θ, counts, and σ when given) is fitted with the calculated patterns of one or more crystal phases. The calculation is the powder pattern of the same physics as CAPS's own: reflections of the cell's reciprocal lattice, Cromer–Mann form factors (or neutron lengths) with a per-phase isotropic B, Lorentz–polarisation, March–Dollase orientation, the Kα doublet and Thompson–Cox–Hastings pseudo-Voigt peaks whose widths follow U, V, W (Gaussian) and X, Y (Lorentzian). Reflection groups (vectors of equal |G|) keep their structure factors while the lattice changes, so the lattice is refined with the cell's equalities kept (a cubic cell stays cubic, right angles stay right). In a Rietveld fit the intensities come from each phase's structure and its scale; in a Pawley fit every reflection group has its own non-negative intensity and no structure is needed. The linear parameters (scales, intensities and a Chebyshev background) are solved exactly at every trial of the nonlinear ones (zero shift, lattice, widths, B, March r), which Levenberg–Marquardt refines. Weight fractions of several phases follow Hill and Howard from the scales, the cells' masses and volumes. Atom positions, occupancies and rigid-body parameters are not refined; an energy-restrained Rietveld variant is not given (no published method to check it against was found).

$$\begin{gathered}y_c(2\theta_i) = b(2\theta_i) + \sum_p S_p \sum_{hkl} m\,|F_{hkl}|^2\,\mathrm{LP}\,P_{\mathrm{MD}}\,\Omega(2\theta_i - 2\theta_{hkl}) \\ S = \sum_i w_i\,(y_i - y_{c,i})^2, \qquad R_{wp} = \sqrt{\frac{S}{\sum_i w_i y_i^2}}, \qquad W_p = \frac{S_p (ZMV)_p}{\sum_i S_i (ZMV)_i}\end{gathered}$$

Indexing a powder pattern and solving a structure in direct space

A measured pattern's peaks are found (local maxima of the smoothed pattern above its rolling-minimum background, positions from a parabola through the top three points, a weaker peak where a stronger one's Kα2 falls dropped). The first lines are indexed by the successive dichotomy method: boxes of cell lengths (and the monoclinic β) are kept while every observed Q = 1/d² lies, within its tolerance, in the range of Q some hkl takes over the box, and are halved until they are 0.02 Å wide; the survivors are refined by least squares on Q, which is linear in the reciprocal metric. The smallest cells are searched first and, once a cell is found, boxes beyond twice its volume (its supercells) are left out. Cells are ranked by the lines they index and de Wolff's M20 (each line's error floored at a tenth of its tolerance, what a peak search can place), with the higher symmetry preferred when merits are within a factor of two per step; Smith and Snyder's F_N is given beside it. A structure is then solved in direct space by simulated annealing of a molecule's position, orientation and torsions in the cell and space group, scored by the profile R_wp of the refinement engine (scale and background solved at every trial), moves bringing atoms of different molecules within a contact distance refused, the moves shrinking as it cools and a downhill polish at the end.

$$\begin{gathered}Q_{hkl} = \frac{1}{d_{hkl}^2} = h^2 A + k^2 B + l^2 C + hl\,D, \qquad M_{20} = \frac{Q_{20}}{2\,\bar\varepsilon\,N_{20}}, \qquad F_N = \frac{1}{\overline{|\Delta 2\theta|}}\,\frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{poss}}} \\ P_{\mathrm{accept}} = \min\left(1,\; e^{-\Delta R_{wp}^2 / T}\right)\end{gathered}$$

Atomic displacement parameters from a trajectory

Each atom is followed through the frames, every step taken at its minimum image so that a crossing of the cell edge is not a jump, and the drift of the cell's mass-weighted centre is removed frame by frame. About the atom's mean position the displacement tensor is U = ⟨Δr Δr^T⟩ (Å²); U_iso is a third of its trace and the Debye–Waller B = 8π² U_iso. The eigenvectors of U are the axes of the thermal ellipsoid, its semi-axes c√λ_k with c fixed by the probability p of finding the atom inside, P(χ²₃ ≤ c²) = p (1.5382 for 50 %). When the trajectory's cell is a supercell of a crystal (given as repeats along a, b, c), the atoms whose mean positions fold onto one place of the unit cell form a site and its U is their mean. For a CIF the tensor is expressed on the normalised reciprocal axes, U_cif = N⁻¹ A⁻¹ U A⁻ᵀ N⁻¹ with A the cell vectors as columns and N = diag(a*, b*, c*); U_eq is recovered from U_cif as ⅓ Σ_ij U^ij a*_i a*_j a_i·a_j. As an analysis the mean U_iso, B per element, the mean anisotropy λmax/λmin and a profile of U_iso along an axis are reported.

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf{U} = \langle \Delta\mathbf{r}\, \Delta\mathbf{r}^{\mathsf T} \rangle, \qquad U_{\mathrm{iso}} = \tfrac13 \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{U}, \qquad B = 8\pi^2 U_{\mathrm{iso}}, \qquad P(\chi^2_3 \le c^2) = p \\ \mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{CIF}} = \mathbf{N}^{-1} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{U} \mathbf{A}^{-\mathsf T} \mathbf{N}^{-1}, \qquad U_{\mathrm{eq}} = \tfrac13 \sum_{ij} U^{ij} a^*_i a^*_j\, \mathbf{a}_i \cdot \mathbf{a}_j\end{gathered}$$

Phonons of a crystal with its force field

Lattice dynamics of a crystal (a polymer crystal, a filler) with the assigned force field. The cell is repeated until each width is at least twice the cut-off; its atoms are first relaxed in that supercell's periodic copies (a cell narrower than twice the cut-off cannot be relaxed alone: it sees one image of each neighbour), by FIRE on the cell's own coordinates with each atom's force averaged over its copies. Each atom of the cell is then displaced ±h along x, y and z and the forces on the whole supercell give the force constants; the acoustic sum rule fixes the self terms. Dielectric, piezoelectric and optical response of a crystal

From the relaxed crystal and its force field's second derivatives at k = 0: the force constants K of the cell's particles (cores and shells) by central differences of the forces with the periodic copies moving together, Ewald sums with conducting boundaries giving the transverse response. The static dielectric tensor sums q_i q_j K⁺ over every particle, the high-frequency tensor over the shells with the cores held (1 for rigid ions), and the refractive indices are the square roots of its principal values. The proper piezoelectric constants of the relaxed ions follow from the forces a homogeneous strain puts on the particles at fixed fractional coordinates (the internal-strain tensor Θ): the particles move by K⁺Θ per unit strain and carry their charges with them; d = e S with the relaxed-ion compliance. With the shells condensed out, the zone-centre modes of the cores and their oscillator strengths give ε(ω) with a damping γ and the normal-incidence reflectivity along the principal axes. The crystal is relaxed first (cell at zero stress, then Newton steps on the particles).

$$\begin{gathered}\varepsilon^{0}_{\alpha\beta} = \delta_{\alpha\beta} + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 V}\sum_{ij} q_i q_j\,(K^{+})_{i\alpha,j\beta}, \qquad e_{\alpha j} = \frac{1}{V}\sum_i q_i\,(K^{+}\Theta)_{i\alpha,j}, \qquad d = e\,S \\ \varepsilon(\omega) = \varepsilon^{\infty} + \sum_m \frac{S_m\,\omega_m^2}{\omega_m^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega}, \qquad R = \left|\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon} - 1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon} + 1}\right|^2\end{gathered}$$

Gale & Rohl 2003; Lyddane, Sachs & Teller 1941 for the one-oscillator check

Crystal habit: BFDH, attachment energy, Wulff shapes

Which faces a crystal shows and the shape it takes. Faces (hkl) up to a largest index are grouped into forms by the crystal's point group and ranked by their effective interplanar spacing: d_hkl of the first order n(hkl) that the space group does not extinguish (an operation with h·R = h and h·t not an integer: lattice centring, screw axes, glide planes), so fcc's {100} counts at a/2. With a force field, the attachment energy of each form is the energy of a growth unit (a molecule, or an ion with its shell) in the bulk less its energy in one slice of thickness d_eff alone, both unrelaxed so intramolecular energy cancels; among the cuts between units the one with the lowest |E_att| is taken. The surface energy is that of an unrelaxed slab several slices thick. Slabs are built on the plane's own two-dimensional lattice with whole units and vacuum along the normal; bonds are carried with their lattice images, so chains along short axes stay whole. A slice with a dipole along its normal (a polar face) or a net charge gets no energy, nor does a face that would cut a covalent chain or network: a polymer crystal's faces parallel to its chains get energies and its shapes are prisms open along the chains (their sides are reported). The shapes are Wulff constructions with distances 1/d_eff (BFDH), |E_att| (growth) or γ (equilibrium); a particle can be carved in any of them, whole units whose centres lie inside.

$$\begin{gathered}d_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{d_{hkl}}{n}, \quad n = \min\{n : (nh, nk, nl)\ \text{not extinguished}\} \qquad E_{\mathrm{att}} = E_{\mathrm{latt}} - E_{\mathrm{slice}} \\ \gamma = \frac{E_{\mathrm{slab}} - N e_{\mathrm{bulk}}}{2A} \qquad \Omega = \bigcap_i \{\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{n}_i\cdot\mathbf{x} \le h_i\}, \quad h_i \propto \frac{1}{d_{\mathrm{eff}}},\ |E_{\mathrm{att}}|\ \text{or}\ \gamma\end{gathered}$$

Donnay & Harker 1937; Hartman & Perdok 1955; Hartman & Bennema 1980; Wulff 1901

Density fields: dynamic mean-field theory of Gaussian chains

Polymer blends and block copolymers as volume-fraction fields on a periodic grid (two- or three-dimensional), the mesoscale method of Fraaije and co-workers. Molecules are Gaussian chains of beads (a sequence such as A8B8); an external potential per bead type gives their densities through the chain propagators, with the bond kernel exp(−q²a²/6) applied by FFT and each step rescaled so strong fields do not underflow. The free energy adds Flory–Huggins χ between types (smoothed by the same kernel) and a Helfand compressibility; the chemical potentials drive conserved Langevin diffusion with local mobility, finite volumes on the cell faces (mass exact) and conserved noise. At each step the potentials are found again for the new densities by Newton steps with the chains' RPA response matrix; the stiff compressibility term is taken implicitly in Fourier space, so its fast mode sets no time-step limit. Results: the fields over time, the order parameter of each type, the χ and compressibility energies and the spacing of the strongest wave.

$$\begin{gathered}q_s = w_s\,(K \star q_{s-1}),\quad \hat K(q) = e^{-q^2a^2/6},\quad \theta_s \propto \frac{q_s\,q^\dagger_s}{w_s},\quad w_s = e^{-U_{t(s)}} \\ \mu_I = -U_I + \sum_J \chi_{IJ}\,(K\star\theta_J) + \kappa_H\Big(\sum_J \theta_J - 1\Big), \qquad \frac{\partial\theta_I}{\partial\tau} = \nabla\cdot(\theta_I\nabla\mu_I) + \nabla\cdot\zeta_I \\ \delta\theta_I = -\sum_J R_{IJ}(q)\,\delta U_J, \qquad R_{IJ}(q) = \sum_m \frac{f_m}{N_m}\sum_{s\in I,\,s'\in J} \hat K(q)^{|s-s'|}\end{gathered}$$

Fraaije 1993; Fraaije et al. 1997; Maurits & Fraaije 1997

Study table: order, filter, colour, histogram and SD files

Tools for the rows of a study table. Rows are ordered by several columns, ties by the next key, each ascending or descending; a value that is not a number goes last either way, and equal rows keep their order. A filter shows the rows that meet a condition on the columns, written with the formula language plus comparisons and and/or. Cells of one column are coloured by their value on a viridis ramp from the column's smallest to its largest value. The chart can show a histogram of a column: bins of equal width over its range, the width from Freedman and Diaconis (2·IQR·n^(−1/3), quartiles by linear interpolation), Scott (3.49·s·n^(−1/3)) or Sturges (⌈log₂ n⌉ + 1 bins). The number of bins is ⌈range/h⌉, the bins then share the range equally, and the last bin is closed on the right. The rows shown that have a structure file can be written as an SD file, one record per row with every column as a data item (a > <name> line, the value, a blank line).

$$h_{\mathrm{FD}} = 2\,\mathrm{IQR}\,n^{-1/3},\qquad h_{\mathrm{Scott}} = 3.49\,s\,n^{-1/3},\qquad k_{\mathrm{Sturges}} = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil + 1,\qquad k = \left\lceil \frac{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}{h} \right\rceil$$

IQR the interquartile range, s the sample standard deviation, n the values with a number
