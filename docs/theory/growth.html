Configurational-bias regrowth of chain ends

A Monte Carlo move that samples chain conformations of a built, typed cell. It picks a chain, one of its free ends and one to a few of the rotatable backbone bonds nearest that end, and regrows the end bond by bond from the inside out: at each bond k torsion angles are drawn uniformly on the circle, each weighted by the Boltzmann factor of the energy of the atoms it places, and one is chosen by its weight. The old end is retraced the same way (its own torsion counted as one of the trials) and the new end is accepted with probability min(1, W_new/W_old). Trial energies are the assigned force field's: van der Waals (shifted to zero at the cut-off, 1-4 pairs scaled), damped shifted force electrostatics and the torsion terms about each regrown bond, class II cross terms included. Bond lengths, angles, impropers and every stereo configuration are untouched; ring, double, aromatic and carbonyl C–N/C–O bonds are held.

$$W = \prod_i \sum_j e^{-\beta u_{ij}}, \qquad P_\mathrm{acc} = \min\left(1,\, \frac{W_\mathrm{new}}{W_\mathrm{old}}\right)$$

Siepmann & Frenkel 1992; Rosenbluth & Rosenbluth 1955

Functional groups on nanotubes, sheets and fillers

Groups grafted covalently onto a filler: the sidewalls, ends and edges of carbon and boron nitride nanotubes and sheets (and any sp² framework), or the atoms chosen. A group is a preset (hydroxyl, carboxyl, amine, methyl, fluoro, phenyl, nitrophenyl, amide, ester, hydroxymethyl, vinyl, thiol, aminopropyl, octadecylamide, PEG) or a SMILES with one attachment point. The sites follow a pattern: at random, every site at a spacing, a band along the axis, a helix round a tube, the tube's rim or the flake's edge hydrogens, or the selected atoms; only the elements named (e.g. boron of h-BN). A sidewall group sits on its site's outward normal — the normal of the three bonds, turned away from the filler's centre (outside a tube, on top of a sheet; inside or below, or both at random, on request); sites stay at least the spacing apart.

$$\text{degree} = \frac{\text{grafted sites}}{\text{framework atoms}} \times 100$$

Chain growth by trial placement

Chains grow unit by unit inside the periodic cell. Each step places the next backbone atom and its substituents from internal coordinates (NeRF) and draws many trials of the free torsions. The draw depends on the bond: between two sp³ atoms trans or gauche± with 15° Gaussian jitter, and in 30 % of draws any angle (crowded units such as methacrylates need the room between); next to an sp² atom 30° steps from the template's angle with 5° jitter; between two sp² atoms (conjugated) 0 or 180° with 8° jitter; ester and amide links trans with 8° jitter. The default method keeps the roomiest trial: the one whose worst distance to any atom already in the cell, minus its contact limit, is largest. Two Rosenbluth methods instead draw one of the trials within the contact limits with probability ∝ exp(−E/kT) at 450 K: method 1 with a soft-sphere overlap energy, method 2 with UFF Lennard-Jones between atoms (cut at 6 Å); both add Jorgensen's butane torsion energy about sp³–sp³ bonds, and the chains' Rosenbluth weights are reported. Units with more than three heavy-atom torsions get twice the trials for each extra one (at most eight times). A poor step backs up a few units; a chain that cannot continue restarts elsewhere.

$$\begin{gathered}\text{best of }k:\ \ \argmax_\text{trials}\ \min_j\left(|r - r_j| - \ell(z, z_j)\,s\right), \qquad \ell(a, b) = 0.88\,(R_a + R_b) - 0.08\,n_\mathrm{H} \\ \text{Rosenbluth: } P(\text{trial}) = \frac{e^{-E/k_\mathrm{B}T}}{\sum_\text{trials} e^{-E/k_\mathrm{B}T}}, \qquad W = \prod_\text{steps}\frac{1}{k}\sum_\text{trials} e^{-E/k_\mathrm{B}T}\end{gathered}$$

CAPS's own scheme; the Rosenbluth methods after Theodorou & Suter 1985 (method 1), without a Monte Carlo acceptance step

Thiolate ligands on metal particles

Surface atoms of a gold (silver, copper, platinum, palladium) particle are those with fewer neighbours than the bulk. Sulfur sits in three-fold hollows of three mutually neighbouring surface atoms, 2.45 Å from each (on top of an atom where the surface has no hollow), sites at least √3 times the metal's nearest-neighbour distance apart — the √3 × √3 R30° packing of thiolates on Au(111). Each ligand's chain is straightened (all anti) and points out of the surface, rolled about its axis away from the others.

$$h = \sqrt{d_\mathrm{MS}^2 - R^2}\ \text{ above the hollow's centre } (R\text{: its distance to the three atoms}), \qquad d_\mathrm{SS} \ge \sqrt{3}\,d_\mathrm{MM}$$

packing as reviewed by Love et al. 2005

Polymer brushes on surfaces

Chains grafted by one end to sites on a fibre or filler surface: a site atom near the top face with a hydrogen pointing up (a silanol's O) loses that hydrogen and the chain's head bonds in its place, leaving along it. Sites are drawn at random at least a spacing apart, at a grafting density σ; the chains grow upward with the same contact rules as any growth.

$$N = \operatorname{round}(\sigma A),\qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{achieved}} = \frac{N}{A}$$

the head–site bond and the atoms within three bonds of it are bonded neighbours, not contacts; the first unit's torsions are referenced to the site's own neighbours (Si–O–C)

Semicrystalline lamellar stacks

A crystal lamella cut from a cell of the polymer crystal library (PE, PVDF, iPP, PEO, POM, nylon 6 and 66, PET, PLLA, natural rubber, PTFE), with an amorphous layer between it and its periodic image holding loops, bridges and tails. The stems run along the crystal's c, the fold surfaces are its ab plane and the stack normal is z, so a monoclinic or triclinic cell gives stems that lean by the angle between c and z. The amorphous segments are grown from the crystal's own repeat unit: the shortest period of its chain (CH2 for PE, CH2-CF2 for PVDF, the whole repeat of nylon 6), each backbone atom with the crystal's bond length, angle and side atoms in its local frame, so the stereochemistry is the crystal's (isotactic iPP, L-lactide). Torsions are held in rings, amides, esters and C=C, take two states between trigonal atoms and come from a trans-gauche set elsewhere. A loop or bridge closes in phase with the stem it enters, and for a chain with a direction (nylon 6, iPP, PLLA, natural rubber) it joins two stems that point opposite ways along the chain. Tails end at an sp3-sp3 bond, capped with H. The force field is chosen per polymer (OPLS-AA 2024 or PCFF, the one that types every atom of the cell).

$$S\,(2 - f_t) = 2N,\qquad P_2 = \left\langle \tfrac{1}{2}\left(3\cos^2\theta - 1\right)\right\rangle$$

N stems have 2N ends; a loop or bridge takes two, a tail one. θ is the angle between a backbone chord r(i+w) − r(i−w) and the stems' direction; w is 1 for a zig-zag and 2 to 5 for a helix, whichever lines the stems up best

Substitutional disorder: quasirandom structures and distinct arrangements

A fraction x of the sites of one element in a periodic cell (usually a supercell made first) take another element; site i carries σ_i = +1 for the original element and −1 for the substituent. Pair shells m are the distinct distances between sites of that sublattice (minimum image), and Π_m is the mean of σ_iσ_j over the pairs of shell m. A truly random alloy has Π_m = (1 − 2x)² on every shell. The special quasirandom structure is the arrangement in the cell whose first shells come closest to that: the weighted sum of |Π_m − (1 − 2x)²| (weight 1/m) is minimised by simulated annealing of swaps between unlike sites, from several random starts, each swap updating the shell sums from the two sites' neighbours only. For a few substituents the arrangements can instead be listed in full: every set of k sites out of n is mapped by each symmetry operation of the cell (the integer rotations of the cell basis that keep its metric, combined with each translation that sends every atom onto an atom of its element), the lexicographically smallest image is kept as the representative, and the orbit sizes, which add up to C(n, k), are the arrangements' weights in a configurational average.

$$\begin{gathered}\Pi_m = \frac{1}{N_m} \sum_{(i<j) \in m} \sigma_i \sigma_j, \qquad \Pi_m^{\mathrm{random}} = (1 - 2x)^2, \qquad f = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{1}{m}\,\left|\Pi_m - (1 - 2x)^2\right| \\ \sum_{\mathrm{orbits}} g = \binom{n}{k}\end{gathered}$$

Zunger, Wei, Ferreira & Bernard 1990; Kirkpatrick et al. 1983

Disorder in CIF files: groups, mixed and split sites, vacancies

A CIF marks disorder with the site occupancy and, for alternative arrangements of several atoms, a disorder assembly and group per site (_atom_site_occupancy, _atom_site_disorder_assembly, _atom_site_disorder_group; group '.' or 0 is ordered). Every site is generated through the space group's operations; partially occupied sites without a group that fall within 0.8 Å of each other are alternatives for one place (split positions, two elements sharing a site), and what their occupancies leave is a vacancy. By default each assembly keeps its group of the largest occupancy and each place its occupant of the largest occupancy (a lone site below 0.5 is left out), which gives one ordered cell with no overlapping atoms. A named group can be kept instead in every assembly. Or a supercell is filled at random to the occupancies: one group is drawn for each copy of an assembly, a copy being one symmetry operation in one cell, worked out from the unwrapped image so that a disordered molecule cut by the cell edge stays whole, and one occupant or a vacancy is drawn for each place. The composition the occupancies give is reported beside the one written.

$$P(g) = \mathrm{occ}(g), \qquad P(j) = \mathrm{occ}_j, \quad P(\varnothing) = 1 - \sum_j \mathrm{occ}_j, \qquad \mathbf{c} = \mathbf{R} - \lfloor \mathbf{f} \rfloor$$

Hall, Allen & Brown 1991 (the CIF data items)

Shaped crystal cuts that keep whole molecules

A particle of a given shape (sphere, cube, octahedron, rod, cone and the rest of the particle builder's shapes) is cut from a crystal by copying the cell along its lattice vectors. Atom by atom, each copied atom is kept when it lies inside the shape. For a molecular crystal the molecules are found first from the bonds with every lattice image (a chain along a short axis bonds an atom to two images of one neighbour), by a flood fill that keeps each atom's lattice offset; each molecule is made whole that way, and every copy of it is kept or left out as a whole by where its centroid falls, so no molecule is cut. A component that meets one of its own atoms at another lattice offset is a periodic network (a polymer chain, a covalent framework) and is still cut atom by atom, with a note. The shape's base can be put on a family of lattice planes: the shape is turned so that its z axis lies along the plane normal h a* + k b* + l c*, and the particle is written with that normal along z.

The space group kept with the structure

A structure keeps the space group it was read with (a CIF's operations, when every operation maps the ordered atoms onto themselves), built in (the crystal builder) or found (the highest group its atoms have within a tolerance, as operations in the structure's own coordinates). A CIF is then written as the asymmetric unit, one atom of every orbit, with the operations, as long as the group still holds after any edits; otherwise as P 1. A supercell or a primitive cell drops the group, because its operations belong to the old cell. The cell editor fits six numbers to the crystal system by averaging the lattice metric over the group's rotations. Moving an atom moves every symmetry image of it. On a special position the move is first projected onto the displacements the site symmetry allows, so the atom stays there.

Copolymers of several units: terminal and penultimate models, drift with conversion

The order of the units along a chain is a Markov chain. In the terminal model the last unit decides the next: P_ij = (f_j / r_ij) / Σ_k (f_k / r_ik), with f the feed and r_ij = k_ii / k_ij the reactivity ratios (r_ii = 1); for two units this is the Mayo–Lewis model, for more the Alfrey–Goldfinger and Walling–Briggs one. A transition matrix can also be given directly. In the penultimate model (two units) the last two decide: r1 = k111/k112 and r1′ = k211/k212 for a chain ending in A, r2 and r2′ for one ending in B. The copolymer composition is the stationary distribution of the chain (solved directly), the dyad and triad fractions are its joint probabilities along the chain, and the number-average run length of a unit a is F_a / Σ_(b≠a) D_ba. In a batch the faster monomer is used up first, so the feed drifts: the unreacted monomer M loses F_i dM of unit i, d f_i / d ln M = F_i − f_i, which is integrated by fourth-order Runge–Kutta in ln M up to the chosen conversion (for two units this reproduces the closed form of Meyer and Lowry). With drift between chains each chain is made at a conversion drawn evenly up to the final one, from the feed left then; with drift along the chain (living growth) position i of a chain belongs to conversion X (i + ½)/DP. The builder reports the composition, dyads, triads and run lengths of the cell it grew against the model.

$$\begin{gathered}P_{ij} = \frac{f_j / r_{ij}}{\sum_k f_k / r_{ik}}, \qquad \mathbf{F}\,\mathsf{P} = \mathbf{F}, \quad \sum_i F_i = 1, \qquad D_{ij} = F_i P_{ij}, \qquad \bar n_a = \frac{F_a}{\sum_{b \ne a} D_{ba}} \\ \frac{d f_i}{d \ln M} = F_i(\mathbf{f}) - f_i\end{gathered}$$

Graft copolymers: grafts grown with the backbone or onto an existing structure

Grafts are side chains of their own repeat units. Grown with the backbone (architecture graft), each backbone unit of the chosen kind carries a graft with a set probability, at most a set number per chain; the graft starts on the unit's atom marked with an atom class of 3 or more in its SMILES ([CH:3], the methine of a natural-rubber unit written [*]CC(C)=[CH:3]C[*]), replacing that atom's hydrogen, or on its head or tail atom when nothing is marked. The grafts have their own sequence kind and length, and grow once their backbone is complete, as comb side chains do. Grafted onto an existing structure (a cell, a chain or a molecule), the sites are atoms picked by number (a hydrogen stands for the atom it sits on), each optionally standing for every atom equivalent to it (same element, hydrogens, heavy neighbours and residue name); each candidate is kept with a probability, at least a set distance from the others, up to a set total. Each site gives up the hydrogen pointing into most room, and a chain grows from there along that hydrogen's direction while the structure is held fixed; a structure without a cell is put in a box with room around it. Molecules are renumbered by connectivity, so a graft belongs to the molecule it hangs on.

$$P(\text{graft on unit } u) = p, \qquad \lvert \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j \rvert \ge d_{\min} \ \text{for kept sites } i, j$$

Rings, inserted atoms and joined pieces

Three sketcher edits that work in 3D. A ring of 3 to 8 atoms is fused onto a picked bond or made spiro on a picked atom. For a fused ring, one hydrogen of each bond atom on the same side makes way, and the new atoms complete a regular polygon with the bond as one side, in the plane of the bond and those hydrogens. For a spiro ring, two hydrogens of the atom make way (or, with one or none, its two open tetrahedral directions). The polygon lies in their plane, symmetric about their bisector, its side the mean covalent sum of the ring bonds. Six- to eight-rings start puckered by ±0.25 Å so the minimiser can leave the plane. The new atoms are written in ring order (O for an epoxide, N C=O C C for a γ-lactam, C C C N with Aromatic for a pyridine ring), hydrogens fill their valences, and UFF relaxes the new atoms with the two bond atoms. An inserted atom X goes into the bond i–j with both new bonds at covalent sums and the angle i–X–j tetrahedral. An open chain gives way, the smaller side moving out along the bond; in a ring, i and j stay and X sits off the bond away from the ring. A piece from another file is joined through two leaving atoms with one bond each, usually hydrogens. The piece is turned so its bond points back along the structure's, the new bond is set at the covalent sum, and the piece is turned about it to the torsion asked or, by default, to the turn with the largest closest contact.

$$R = \frac{L}{2\sin(\pi/n)},\qquad a = \frac{L}{2\tan(\pi/n)},\qquad d_{ij} = \sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2 - 2 r_1 r_2 \cos\theta_t},\quad \cos\theta_t = -\tfrac{1}{3},\qquad r_{AB} = r_A + r_B$$

n ring atoms; L the polygon side; R its circumradius and a its apothem; r₁ = r_i + r_X and r₂ = r_X + r_j from the covalent radii; d_ij the distance i–j after an insertion into an open chain

Dendrimers and hyperbranched molecules from AB_n units

A branching unit is a SMILES with its A site written [*:1] and its n B sites written [*:2]. A core has B sites only, an end group one A site, and a focal group one B site for the first unit when there is no core. Units are joined site to site in 3D: the two site atoms leave, the new bond is set at the covalent sum, and each unit is turned about it to the clearest position (the join of the ring tools). UFF then relaxes the molecule. A perfect dendrimer fills every B site, shell after shell. Slow monomer addition adds the units one at a time, each onto a B site drawn uniformly from all open ones, on the core too. Flory's statistics of AB_n polycondensation (equal reactivity, no cycles) make a molecule, seen from its focal unit, a Galton–Watson tree in which each B site reacts independently. A molecule of exactly N units is that tree conditioned on its size. It is drawn exactly: the site occupations are redrawn until N − 1 sites are used, and the sequence is rotated by the cycle lemma into a tree. The report gives the units per shell and by the number of B sites used. For AB₂ it also gives Frey's and Fréchet's degrees of branching. Presets: PAMAM on ethylenediamine (generation g has g + 1 shells), PPI on 1,4-diaminobutane (generation g has g shells) and bis-MPA polyester on trimethylolpropane.

$$\mathrm{DB}_{\mathrm{Frey}} = \frac{2D}{2D + L},\qquad \mathrm{DB}_{\mathrm{Fr\acute{e}chet}} = \frac{D + T}{D + T + L},\qquad \frac{dT}{dN} = 1 - \frac{2T}{F},\quad \frac{dL}{dN} = \frac{2T - L}{F},\quad \frac{dD}{dN} = \frac{L}{F}$$

D, L, T: AB₂ units with 2, 1, 0 B sites carrying a unit (end groups do not count); the limits are for large N. In slow addition each new unit turns a T into an L with chance 2T/F or an L into a D with chance L/F (F = 2T + L open sites, growing by one per unit), so the fractions settle at 1/3 each
